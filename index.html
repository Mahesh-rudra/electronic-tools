Ohm's law and power

Fill in any two. The other two are calculated.

How it works

Voltage is the electrical "pressure" that pushes charge around a circuit, current is the flow of that charge, and resistance is how strongly a component opposes the flow. Ohm's law ties the three together: double the voltage across a resistor and the current doubles too.

V = I × R
I = V / R
R = V / I

Whenever current flows through resistance, energy turns into heat. That power can be written three ways, depending on which two quantities you know:

P = V × I = I² × R = V² / R

Practical tip: choose a resistor whose power rating is at least twice the power it will dissipate. A 1/4 W resistor carrying 0.2 W will run hot and drift over time.

Series and parallel

Enter values separated by commas or spaces.

How it works

Resistors and inductors in series simply add, because the current has to pass through each one in turn. In parallel, the current has several paths, so the total is always smaller than the smallest single part.

Series: R = R1 + R2 + …
Parallel: 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + …

Capacitors work the other way round. In parallel their plate areas add, so capacitance adds. In series it is like moving the plates further apart, so the total drops below the smallest one.

Parallel: C = C1 + C2 + …
Series: 1/C = 1/C1 + 1/C2 + …

Two equal resistors in parallel give exactly half the value, which is a handy trick when you don't have the right part in your box.

Current divider

Two resistors in parallel sharing a total current.

How it works

A voltage divider is two resistors in series across a supply. The same current flows through both, so the voltage splits in proportion to the resistances. The output is taken across the bottom resistor R2.

Vout = Vin × R2 / (R1 + R2)

The formula only holds when whatever you connect to Vout draws very little current. A load in parallel with R2 lowers the effective R2 and pulls Vout down. A common rule is to make the divider current at least ten times the load current. Dividers are great for scaling a voltage into a microcontroller's ADC or setting a bias point, but they should not be used to power anything; use a regulator for that.

A current divider is the mirror image: two resistors in parallel share a current, and the smaller resistor takes the larger share.

I1 = Itotal × R2 / (R1 + R2)

Op-amp active filter

Second-order filter, −40 dB per decade. Pick one capacitor value and the calculator chooses the rest. Q = 0.707 gives the flattest (Butterworth) response.

How it works

A passive RC filter rolls off at only −20 dB per decade, and its corner is soft. Adding an op-amp lets two resistors and two capacitors make a second-order filter that rolls off at −40 dB per decade, with a corner shape you can choose through Q.

f₀ = 1 / (2π √(R1 R2 C1 C2))

Q sets the shape around the cutoff. Q = 0.707 (Butterworth) is the flattest passband with no peak. Q = 0.577 (Bessel) gives the cleanest step response with no overshoot. Higher Q makes a sharper corner with a peak in the response.

Sallen-Key uses the op-amp as a unity-gain buffer, so it is simple and keeps the signal in phase. For the low-pass version, C1 must be at least 4Q² times C2, so the calculator picks the next standard C1 and then solves for the two resistors.

Multiple feedback (MFB) uses the op-amp as an inverting amplifier, so it can add gain and is less sensitive to component tolerance, but it inverts the signal.

Resistors are rounded to E96 and capacitors to E12, and the actual cutoff and Q are recalculated with those values. Use 1% resistors and C0G/NP0 or film capacitors, and choose an op-amp with gain-bandwidth at least 100 × gain × f₀.

dBm, watts and volts

Voltages assume a sine wave across the given impedance.

How it works

Decibels describe a ratio on a logarithmic scale, which turns huge ranges into small numbers and lets gains in a chain simply add up.

dB = 10 × log₁₀(P2 / P1)
dB = 20 × log₁₀(V2 / V1)

Voltage uses 20 instead of 10 because power goes with voltage squared. So doubling the voltage is +6 dB, while doubling the power is +3 dB. Both describe the same change when the impedance stays the same.

dBm is power compared with 1 milliwatt: 0 dBm = 1 mW, 30 dBm = 1 W, and −30 dBm = 1 µW. To turn dBm into volts you need the impedance, usually 50 Ω in RF work or 600 Ω in old audio systems.

P = 1 mW × 10^(dBm/10)
V_rms = √(P × R)

dBV is voltage compared with 1 V RMS, and dBµV compared with 1 µV, common in EMC testing.

RMS, peak and average

Works for voltage or current. Enter any one value.

How it works

RMS (root mean square) is the DC value that would heat a resistor by the same amount. When people say mains is 230 V, they mean 230 V RMS, and the peak is actually about 325 V.

Sine: V_rms = V_peak / √2 ≈ 0.707 × V_peak
Triangle: V_rms = V_peak / √3
Square: V_rms = V_peak
Pulse: V_rms = V_peak × √D

A symmetric wave averages to zero over a full cycle, so for sine, square and triangle waves the average here means the rectified average, the average of its absolute value. For a sine that is 2/π ≈ 0.637 × peak.

Crest factor is peak ÷ RMS, and form factor is RMS ÷ average. Many cheap multimeters measure the average and multiply by 1.111, the sine wave's form factor. That reads correctly for sine waves but wrong for anything else, which is why true-RMS meters exist.

RC time constant

A capacitor charging through a resistor. Target voltage is optional.

How it works

When a capacitor charges through a resistor, it charges quickly at first and then more and more slowly, because the voltage left across the resistor (which drives the current) keeps shrinking. The speed is set by the time constant τ (tau).

τ = R × C
V(t) = Vs × (1 − e^(−t/τ))

After one τ the capacitor reaches 63.2% of the supply, after 3τ about 95%, and after 5τ over 99%, which engineers treat as fully charged. Discharging follows the same curve in reverse.

RC timing is used for switch debouncing, power-on reset delays, simple timers and smoothing. Note that electrolytic capacitors often have a ±20% tolerance, so the timing will be equally loose. Use film or ceramic capacitors where accuracy matters.

555 timer

How it works

The 555 contains two comparators, a flip-flop and a discharge transistor. Its comparators watch the capacitor voltage against thresholds at 1/3 and 2/3 of Vcc.

In astable mode, C charges through R1 + R2 until it reaches 2/3 Vcc. The output then goes low and pin 7 discharges C through R2 alone, until it falls to 1/3 Vcc and the cycle repeats. This gives a continuous square wave.

tHigh = 0.693 (R1 + R2) C
tLow = 0.693 R2 C
f = 1.44 / ((R1 + 2R2) C)

Because charging goes through both resistors but discharging through only one, the duty cycle is always above 50%. A diode across R2 bypasses it during charging and allows lower duty cycles.

In monostable mode, a brief low pulse on pin 2 (below 1/3 Vcc) starts one output pulse of fixed length, after which the timer waits for the next trigger.

t = 1.1 R C

The 10 nF capacitor on pin 5 filters noise on the internal threshold. CMOS versions like the 7555 or TLC555 draw far less current and suit battery projects.

LED series / parallel designer

Typical Vf: red 2.0 V, yellow 2.1 V, green, blue and white 3.0–3.3 V.

How it works

An LED has a nearly fixed forward voltage (Vf) once it conducts, and above that point a tiny voltage increase causes a huge current increase. So an LED must always have something limiting its current. The simplest limiter is a series resistor, which drops the leftover voltage.

R = (Vsupply − n × Vf) / I

Putting several LEDs in series is efficient: they share one current and one resistor, and less power is wasted. But their Vf values add up, so the supply limits how many fit in one string. Leave some headroom across the resistor, because with very little voltage left, small supply changes cause large brightness changes.

For more LEDs, use several strings in parallel, each with its own resistor. Never share one resistor between parallel LEDs: their Vf differs slightly, so one LED takes most of the current and burns out first. Typical indicator LEDs run at 5–20 mA. Brightness is set by current, not voltage.

Battery pack calculator

Cells in series add voltage. Cells in parallel add capacity.

How it works

Connecting cells in series adds their voltages, while the capacity (Ah) stays that of one cell. Connecting them in parallel adds capacity and current ability, while the voltage stays that of one cell. Packs are described as "xSyP": 4S2P means two parallel groups, four of them in series, eight cells in all.

V pack = S × V cell
Capacity = P × cell Ah
Energy (Wh) = V × Ah

A Li-ion cell is about 3.6–3.7 V nominal, 4.2 V when full and around 3.0 V when empty. Charge current is often expressed as a C-rate: 0.5C on a 5 Ah pack is 2.5 A.

Safety matters: use identical cells from the same batch, only connect parallel cells when they are at the same voltage, and always use a BMS (battery management system). It prevents over-charge, over-discharge and over-current, and balances the series groups so none drifts out of range.

Li-ion battery runtime

For devices that sleep, enter the sleep current and the share of time spent active.

How it works

Runtime is simply how much charge you can use divided by how fast you use it.

Runtime (h) = usable capacity (Ah) / average current (A)

Never plan on 100% of the rated capacity. Cells lose capacity with age, in cold weather and at high currents, and stopping before fully empty extends their life. 80% is a sensible planning figure.

Many devices, such as sensors and Bluetooth tags, are asleep most of the time and wake briefly. Their average current is what matters:

I avg = I active × duty + I sleep × (1 − duty)

At low duty cycles the sleep current often dominates the result, which is why low-power designs focus so much on microamps of sleep current. If the load runs through a switching converter, the battery current is the load power divided by (battery voltage × efficiency).

Wire resistance

AWG accepts 0–40 and 1/0 to 4/0. Round trip counts the return wire too.

How it works

A wire's resistance grows with its length and shrinks with its cross-sectional area. Each material has a resistivity ρ; copper is about 1.68 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m at 20 °C.

R = ρ × L / A

Doubling the length doubles the resistance, while doubling the diameter quarters it, because area grows with the square of the diameter. In the AWG system, a smaller number means a thicker wire, and every 3 gauges roughly halves or doubles the area.

Current must flow out to the load and back again, so for a DC circuit count the length both ways (round trip). Resistance also rises with temperature: copper gains about 0.39% per °C.

The resistance causes a voltage drop (V = I × R) and wastes power as heat (P = I² × R). For low-voltage circuits like 5 V or 12 V, keep the drop under about 3–5%, otherwise the load sees noticeably less voltage.

Reactance and impedance

Series circuit. Leave R, L or C blank to leave it out. Voltage is optional.

How it works

Inductors and capacitors oppose alternating current, but unlike resistors, how much they oppose it depends on frequency. This opposition is called reactance (X), measured in ohms.

XL = 2πfL (rises with frequency)
XC = 1 / (2πfC) (falls with frequency)

An inductor resists changes in current, so it blocks high frequencies. A capacitor passes changes easily, so it blocks low frequencies and DC. Their reactances also push the current out of step with the voltage: an inductor makes current lag by 90°, a capacitor makes it lead by 90°. So resistance and reactance combine like the sides of a right-angled triangle, not by simple addition.

|Z| = √(R² + (XL − XC)²)
φ = atan((XL − XC) / R)

When XL equals XC they cancel. This is resonance, at f0 = 1 / (2π√LC). The impedance drops to just R and current peaks. At resonance the voltages across L and C can be many times the supply voltage, even though they cancel each other out.

Filter designer

How it works

A filter lets some frequencies through and weakens others. In an RC low-pass filter, the capacitor acts as an open circuit at low frequencies (signal passes) and as a short to ground at high frequencies (signal is shunted away). Swap R and C and you get a high-pass filter.

RC: fc = 1 / (2πRC)
RL: fc = R / (2πL)

The cutoff frequency fc is where the output has fallen to 70.7% of the input voltage, which is −3 dB or half the power. Beyond it, a single-stage filter falls by 20 dB per decade, meaning ten times the frequency gives one tenth of the voltage. For a steeper drop, stack stages or use an active filter.

A series RLC band-pass filter peaks at its resonant frequency. Its sharpness is the quality factor Q: a high Q means a narrow, selective peak, as used in radio tuning.

f0 = 1 / (2π√LC)
Q = (1/R)√(L/C)
Bandwidth = f0 / Q

Real filters are affected by what drives and loads them. Keep the source impedance low and the load impedance at least ten times R.

Op-amp gain

Ideal op-amp. Target gain suggests an E96 (1%) feedback resistor.

How it works

An op-amp has very high gain, so with negative feedback (output connected back to the − input) it adjusts its output until both inputs sit at the same voltage. Two "golden rules" for an ideal op-amp make the maths easy: no current flows into the inputs, and the inputs are at equal voltage.

Inverting: the + input is grounded, so the − input becomes a "virtual ground". The input current Vin/R1 must all flow through Rf, giving an inverted output.

Gain = −Rf / R1

Non-inverting: the signal goes to the + input. Rf and Rg form a divider that feeds back a fraction of the output. Its input impedance is very high.

Gain = 1 + Rf / Rg

Differential amplifies only the difference between two inputs, rejecting what they share (such as noise). It needs matched pairs: R1 = R3 and R2 = R4. Summing adds several inputs, each weighted by Rf/Rn, like an audio mixer.

Differential: Vout = (R2/R1)(V2 − V1)
Summing: Vout = −Rf (V1/R1 + V2/R2)

Real op-amps can't swing their output past the supply rails, and their bandwidth shrinks as gain rises (bandwidth ≈ gain-bandwidth product ÷ gain). Resistors in the 1 kΩ–100 kΩ range work well for most designs.

Zener regulator

Picks the series resistor so the Zener still regulates at the lowest input and full load.

How it works

A Zener diode is designed to be used in reverse. Once the reverse voltage reaches its Zener voltage Vz, it conducts and holds the voltage nearly constant, absorbing whatever current is needed to do so. A series resistor Rs drops the difference between the input and Vz.

Rs = (Vin min − Vz) / (I load + Iz min)

Rs is sized for the worst case on the supply side, which is the lowest input voltage at full load, while still leaving the minimum current (Iz min, a few mA) for the Zener to stay in regulation. The worst case for the Zener's heat is the opposite: highest input with no load, when all the current flows through the diode.

Pz (worst) = Vz × (Vin max − Vz) / Rs

Zener regulators are simple and fine for voltage references or loads of a few tens of mA, but they waste power constantly. For larger currents, add a transistor as a follower or use a regulator IC.

Linear regulator heat

Presets use typical datasheet values. Check the datasheet of your exact part and package.

How it works

A linear regulator (7805, AMS1117, LM317) works like a variable resistor that adjusts itself to hold the output steady. All the extra voltage is burned off as heat.

P = (Vin − Vout) × I + Vin × Iq
Efficiency ≈ Vout / Vin

That heat has to travel from the silicon junction to the surrounding air. Each step in the path has a thermal resistance θ in °C/W, which works like electrical resistance: more watts means a bigger temperature rise.

Tj = Tambient + P × θJA
With heatsink: θJA → θJC + θCS + θSA

Keep the junction well below its maximum (usually 125 °C), ideally with a 25 °C margin. If it overheats, the regulator's thermal protection shuts it down. A heatsink with a lower θSA (°C/W) removes heat faster.

A regulator also needs a minimum gap between input and output, called dropout, to keep regulating. If the voltage drop is large, for example 12 V to 3.3 V, a switching buck converter is far more efficient.

MOSFET power loss and heatsink

Take RDS(on), rise and fall times and thermal values from your MOSFET's datasheet. For a simple on/off switch, set frequency to 0.

How it works

A MOSFET used as a switch loses power in two main ways. While it is on, current flows through its on-resistance RDS(on), which turns into heat. This is the conduction loss, and it only happens for the fraction of time the MOSFET is on (the duty cycle D).

P_cond = I² × RDS(on) × hot factor × D

RDS(on) rises as the MOSFET warms up, often to 1.5 to 2 times its 25 °C value at 100 to 125 °C. The datasheet has a graph of this, so use the hot factor to stay realistic.

Every time the MOSFET turns on or off, there is a short moment when it carries current and blocks voltage at the same time. That is the switching loss, and it grows with frequency:

P_sw ≈ ½ × V × I × (tr + tf) × f
P_coss = ½ × Coss × V² × f

The second line is the energy stored in the output capacitance Coss, which is dumped inside the MOSFET at each turn-on. Faster edges and lower frequency cut switching loss, while a MOSFET with lower RDS(on) cuts conduction loss. Often you trade one against the other, because bigger MOSFETs have lower RDS(on) but more capacitance.

The gate drive power, Qg × Vgs × f, is burned in the gate driver and gate resistor, not in the MOSFET, so it is shown separately.

Heatsink needed: θSA ≤ (Tj target − Ta) / P − θJC − θCS

The target here is 25 °C below the maximum junction temperature. Rise and fall times in datasheets are measured with a specific gate resistor and driver, so treat the switching loss as an estimate and check the real temperature on your board.

Thermal resistance, junction to ambient

Add up the heat path from the chip to the air and find the temperature at each point.

How it works

Heat flows from the hot silicon junction out to the surrounding air, much like current flows through resistors. Temperature plays the part of voltage, power (W) the part of current, and each step in the path has a thermal resistance θ in °C/W.

Tj = Ta + P × (θJC + θCS + θSA)

θJC is junction to case, fixed by the package. θCS is case to sink, set by whatever sits between the part and the heatsink: thermal grease, a pad or an insulator. θSA is sink to ambient, from the heatsink's datasheet. Without a heatsink, the datasheet gives a single θJA for the whole path.

A thermal pad's resistance depends on its thickness, its conductivity k and the area it covers:

θpad = thickness / (k × area)

Silicone pads are typically 1 to 6 W/m·K, and thermal grease is used in very thin layers, so it usually has the lowest θCS. This formula gives the bulk value only. Real contact surfaces add a little extra, so pad datasheets often list a measured θ that is a better guide.

Aim to keep the junction at least 25 °C below its maximum. Every 10 °C cooler roughly doubles the life of many components.

Current sense resistor

Typical full-scale sense voltage is 50 to 100 mV for a current-sense amplifier.

How it works

A current sense resistor (a shunt) is a small, precise resistor placed in the current path. The voltage across it is proportional to the current, so measuring that voltage tells you the current.

R = Vsense / Imax
P = I² × R

There is a trade-off. A larger sense voltage is easier to measure accurately, but it wastes more power and takes voltage away from the load. That is why 50 to 100 mV full scale is common, with an amplifier to boost it for the ADC. The calculator rounds down to a standard value so the sense voltage stays within your chosen full scale.

For milliohm values, use a resistor made for current sensing, with a low temperature coefficient (TCR), and connect the measurement traces right at the resistor pads (a Kelvin or 4-wire connection). Otherwise the copper trace resistance adds error. Choose a power rating at least twice the dissipation.

PCB trace width (IPC-2221)

Valid for up to about 35 A, 10–100 °C rise and widths up to 400 mil. Length is optional.

How it works

A PCB trace is a flat strip of copper. As current flows it heats up, and a trace that is too narrow can overheat, lift off the board or burn open. The IPC-2221 standard gives an empirical formula linking current, allowed temperature rise and cross-section area:

I = k × ΔT^0.44 × A^0.725
(A in mil², k = 0.048 outer, 0.024 inner)

The width then comes from the area and the copper thickness, given in ounces per square foot: 1 oz copper is about 35 µm (1.38 mil) thick. Thicker copper allows narrower traces for the same current.

Outer-layer traces shed heat into the air, while inner-layer traces are buried in insulating fibreglass, so they need roughly 2.6 times the width. A 10 °C rise is a common, conservative design choice.

IPC-2221 is conservative; the newer IPC-2152 often allows narrower traces. For high currents, use copper pours instead of thin traces, and several vias when changing layers.

Resistor color code

—

Value to bands

Uses the band count selected above (4 or 5 bands).

How it works

Through-hole resistors are too small for printed numbers, so their value is coded in coloured bands. Each colour stands for a digit from 0 to 9: black, brown, red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet, grey, white.

On a 4-band resistor, the first two bands are digits, the third is the multiplier (how many zeros to add) and the fourth is the tolerance. 5-band precision resistors use three digit bands. A 6th band gives the temperature coefficient in ppm/K.

Yellow Violet Red Gold = 4, 7, ×100, ±5% = 4.7 kΩ ±5%

Read from the end where the bands sit closer to the edge. Gold or silver is always the tolerance band, so it goes on the right. Resistor values come from standard E-series sets: E24 (24 values per decade) for 5% parts and E96 for 1% parts, which is why you'll find 4.7 kΩ but not 5 kΩ.

SMD resistor code

3-digit (103), 4-digit (1002), R notation (4R7, R10) or EIA-96 (01C).

Capacitor code

3-digit code in pF, with optional tolerance letter (104K, 225J, 4n7).

How it works

SMD resistors use printed codes instead of colours. In the 3-digit code, the first two digits are the value and the third is the number of zeros: 472 = 47 followed by two zeros = 4700 Ω. The 4-digit code used on 1% parts works the same way with three value digits: 1002 = 100 followed by two zeros = 10 kΩ.

An R marks the decimal point for small values: 4R7 = 4.7 Ω and R10 = 0.1 Ω. A code of 000 or 0 is a zero-ohm jumper.

Tiny 1% resistors use the EIA-96 code: two digits pick a value from the E96 table, and a letter is the multiplier. For example, 01C = 100 × 100 = 10 kΩ.

Ceramic and film capacitors use a similar 3-digit code in picofarads: 104 = 10 followed by four zeros = 100,000 pF = 100 nF = 0.1 µF. A letter after it gives the tolerance: J = ±5%, K = ±10%, M = ±20%. Small SMD capacitors are usually not marked at all, so keep them in labelled packaging.
